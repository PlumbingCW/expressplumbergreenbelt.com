Installing a Sump Pump in Greenbelt, MD: Steps, Rules and Common Mistakes

Installing a sump pump means digging a pit at the lowest point of the basement or crawl space, setting a pump in a lined basin and running a discharge pipe to a safe outfall. The pump part is easy. The pit location and the discharge are what decide whether it works.

This guide covers the sequence, the local rules to check and the mistakes we repair most often.

The installation sequence

1

Choose the low point.

Water collects at the lowest spot. Mark where seepage appears and where the floor slopes.

2

Cut and dig the pit.

A section of slab is cut and a pit is dug to fit the liner basin.

3

Set the basin.

A perforated basin sits in gravel so groundwater can enter it.

4

Place the pump.

The pump sits on a firm base with the float clear of the walls.

5

Run the discharge.

A pipe leaves the basin through a check valve and runs to the outfall.

6

Test.

Pour water into the pit and watch the float, the pump and the check valve.

Where the discharge should go

Prince George's County's Residential Drainage Manual says the outfall should be at least 8 to 10 feet from the foundation wall, and, on private property, at least 10 feet from the property line. It also says to avoid steep slopes at the outfall and not to discharge onto neighboring properties or sidewalks.

It describes discharge into the street right-of-way across a sidewalk or curb as a safety hazard, and says discharging within County right-of-way is illegal. Plan the pipe route on paper before you cut the slab.

Pump size and the backup question

A pump is rated by how much water it moves at a given height. A home with a slow seep needs less than a home with a high water table. Oversizing wastes money and shortens the pump's life by cycling it too hard.

A backup is the part people skip and regret. Heavy storms and power cuts arrive together, so a battery or water-powered backup keeps the pit under control when the main pump cannot run.

Before you dig: utilities and permits

The County manual says to call Miss Utility at 1-800-257-7777 before any digging, and to contact the County's permits office before remedial work. The City of Greenbelt adds that projects needing a county building permit must also be permitted by the City.

A pump discharge must never be connected to the sanitary sewer. WSSC lists storm water and groundwater among prohibited discharges to its sewer system, and a connection overloads the sewer and can back up your neighbors.

The check valve and why it must be accessible

A check valve stops water from flowing back into the pit after the pump shuts off. Without one, the same water cycles in and out, wearing the pump. Install it above the pump with a union or no-hub coupling so it can be replaced without cutting pipe.

Drill a small weep hole in the discharge pipe below the check valve, which prevents airlock. It sprays a small amount into the pit when the pump runs and is normal.

Alarms, lids and radon

A high-water alarm in the pit gives early warning if the pump fails. Choose a battery-powered unit that sounds in the house. A sealed lid keeps debris out and reduces humidity and odor.

If a radon system is planned or already installed, tell the installer. A sealed lid and a pit that is part of a radon system need a certain design, and an open pit can draw soil gas into the house.

Maintenance schedule

Every few months, pour a bucket of water into the pit to test that the float rises and the pump runs. Once a year, remove debris from the pit and inspect the discharge line and the outfall. Replace a battery backup every few years, per the manufacturer.

If the pump runs more often than before, something has changed. It may be a higher water table, a broken downspout or a failing check valve. Call us to find out.

Deciding whether to hire it out

A pump on a basement with an existing pit is a manageable swap. A new pit, a cut slab, a discharge line through a wall and an electrical connection are more than most homeowners want to do themselves. A licensed plumber knows the code, brings the right tools and can finish in a day.

If you do hire it out, ask what is included: the basin, the pump, the check valve, the discharge pipe, the wall penetration, the electrical connection, the lid and the cleanup. Ask who handles the permit.

What to do with an old pit

If the house already has a pit, inspect it before replacing the pump. Check for cracks, silt and a missing lid. A pit that is too shallow or too narrow for the new pump should be replaced. A clogged weep hole or a collapsed liner causes the pump to run constantly.

Photograph the old setup before you remove anything so you can reconnect the discharge in the same place.

What this means for a Greenbelt property

WSSC Water serves Greenbelt and says most plumbing in the county must be done by a WSSC Water-licensed plumber. A sump pump with a discharge line is plumbing work, and an inspector may be needed. Ask for the plumber's license number and verify it with WSSC at 301-206-8588.

Greenbelt Homes, Inc. reports that only a limited number of its homes have basements, so if you live in a cooperative home, check with the cooperative before cutting the slab or running a discharge pipe across common ground. The same discharge rules apply to houses we visit in Lanham and Beltsville, so plan the outfall before you cut the slab.
