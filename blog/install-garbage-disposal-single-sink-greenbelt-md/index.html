How to Install a Garbage Disposal in a Single Sink: What Greenbelt, MD Homes Need to Know

Installing a garbage disposal in a single-bowl sink is a manageable job if you have a power outlet under the sink and a standard drain. The steps are removing the old strainer, mounting the new unit, connecting the discharge to the trap and wiring or plugging it in.

Most problems come after the install: a trap that no longer fits, a dishwasher drain that was not connected or a unit with no power. This guide covers what to check first.

  • Mounting and sealing
  • Trap and discharge
  • Dishwasher connection
  • Power and switch

Check these before you buy a disposal

  • Is there a switched outlet or a wall switch, or do you need an electrician?
  • Is the sink a standard 3.5 inch drain opening?
  • How much cabinet space is below, including room for the trap?
  • Is a dishwasher connected to the sink drain?
  • Does your building or cooperative allow disposals?

Installation steps

1

Power off and clear the cabinet.

Switch off the circuit, remove the old strainer or unit and clear the space.

2

Mount the flange.

Seat the sink flange with plumber's putty and tighten the mounting ring from below.

3

Hang the disposal.

Lift the unit onto the mounting ring and lock it into place.

4

Connect the discharge.

Attach the drain elbow and the trap. If a dishwasher is connected, remove the knockout plug first.

5

Wire or plug in.

Connect to power through a cord or hard-wire according to the code and manual.

6

Test.

Run water, switch on the unit and check every joint for leaks.

The knockout plug: the mistake that floods a kitchen

Many disposals have a small plug inside the dishwasher inlet. If you connect the dishwasher hose without removing the plug, the dishwasher cannot drain and will back up into the machine. The fix is to knock the plug out with a screwdriver and hammer, then fish it out of the chamber.

Do this before you hang the unit. It is much harder once the disposal is mounted and full of water.

Trap geometry and the drain

A disposal sits lower than a standard strainer, so the trap often has to move. The discharge must slope down toward the wall, and the trap needs a proper P-shape with a vent connection. A flat or backward-sloping line holds water and smells.

In older Greenbelt kitchens the drain stub in the wall can be corroded or at an odd height. We check the stub before we hang the unit, since fixing it afterward means taking the disposal back off.

Choosing a disposal for your household

Power matters less than build. A unit with a stainless steel grinding chamber and a multi-stage grind lasts longer than a plastic-lined one. If you cook often or have a large family, choose a higher horsepower model. For a small kitchen with light use, a third horsepower unit is enough.

Noise is a fair concern in an apartment. Models with insulation around the motor are quieter. A continuous-feed model runs while the switch is on, while a batch-feed model runs only when the stopper is in place, which some households prefer for safety.

Wiring and the electrical box

Disposals are usually powered through a cord to a switched outlet under the sink, or hard-wired to a switch on the wall. If there is no outlet or switch, an electrician should add one. Do not run an extension cord to a disposal.

Check that the circuit is rated for the load. A disposal on a circuit shared with a dishwasher and a microwave can trip a breaker. We note this on the visit and tell you if an electrician is needed.

After the installation: how to use it well

Run cold water before, during and after each use. Feed scraps in slowly. Avoid grease, bones, coffee grounds in quantity and stringy vegetables. Grind a handful of ice or citrus peel now and then to clean the chamber.

If it jams, switch it off first. Then use the hex key in the bottom socket to free it, and press the reset button. Never put a hand into the chamber.

Common questions after the install

A new disposal may hum for a moment when started, and it may smell of rubber the first few days. Both are normal. If water seeps at the flange a day later, retighten the mounting ring evenly. If the unit trips the breaker, stop and have the wiring checked.

Run the unit for a few seconds with water each day for a week to settle the seals and clear any residue. If you notice a slow sink drain, check the trap, since the new disposal may have changed its slope.

Mistakes we are called to fix

The most common are a missing knockout plug, a trap installed backward, a loose mounting ring, a disposal wired to a circuit that trips and a drain stub that does not line up. Each is simple to fix, but each means returning to a finished kitchen.

Measure twice and test everything before you close the cabinet. If you hire an installer, ask for a test with water and with the dishwasher running.

What the local sewer system asks of you

A disposal does not make grease safe for the drain. WSSC Water's guidance is to pour grease into a can, put a lid on it, cool it in the refrigerator and toss it in the trash when full. WSSC says grease poured down drains builds up and can cause sewer backups into your home. Free can lids are available on WSSC's Can the Grease page.

WSSC says most plumbing in Prince George's County must be done by a WSSC Water-licensed plumber, and it suggests verifying the license by calling 301-206-8588. In a cooperative or condominium, check the rules on disposals first, since older drain lines can limit what a building allows. We install disposals for apartment and house kitchens in College Park and Lanham, after checking the drain stub and the building rules.
