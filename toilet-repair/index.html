Toilet overflowing: what to do

The first minute matters most. These steps limit the damage.

  • Close the shutoff valve behind the toilet, turning it clockwise.
  • Lift the tank lid and push the flapper down if water is still entering the bowl.
  • Put towels around the base and move rugs out of the way.
  • If other drains are also backing up, stop using all water and call us.

Overflow water can contain waste. Wear gloves and disinfect the floor afterward.

Toilet problems and their usual causes

Constant running or refilling.

A worn flapper, fill valve or chain is leaking water into the bowl.

Weak or slow flush.

Clogged rim jets or a partial blockage.

Rocking toilet.

A loose closet bolt or a failed wax ring.

Water at the base.

A failed wax ring, flange or supply line.

Repeated clogs.

A restriction in the trap or the drain line.

A running toilet and your WSSC Water bill

A toilet that leaks water from the tank into the bowl may not make noise, so you could pay for it for months. WSSC Water bills water and sewer from the meter, and a silent leak raises both.

A quick test is to add a few drops of food coloring to the tank. If the color shows in the bowl after twenty minutes without flushing, the flapper is leaking. Replacing a flapper is one of the cheapest plumbing repairs there is.

Wax rings and flanges in older homes

In any older home a closet flange can be cast iron, clay or plastic. A flange that has rusted or cracked will not hold a toilet firmly, even with a new wax ring.

If your toilet rocks and a new ring does not stop it, the flange may be the issue. We inspect it and repair it with a repair ring when the pipe underneath is sound.

Low-flow toilets and clogs

Toilets sold in the United States since the 1990s use at most 1.6 gallons per flush, and newer models use less. A well-designed low-flow toilet clears waste with a strong siphon. A poorly designed one clogs often and gets blamed for problems that belong to the drain.

If your toilet clogs more than once a month, test it by flushing a normal load. If the bowl empties slowly or gurgles, the drain may be restricted. A camera run shows whether roots, scale or a belly is the cause, and sewer camera inspection covers that.

Supply lines, shutoff valves and tank hardware

The braided supply line that connects the shutoff valve to the tank is a wear item. A line that is kinked, corroded at the nut or older than a decade can burst and flood a bathroom. We replace it when we repair the fill valve, since both sit in the same space.

The shutoff valve is the other weak point. Old multi-turn valves can seize or weep at the stem. A quarter-turn valve is easier to close in an emergency, and replacing it is cheap when the wall is already open. Test yours today by closing and opening it. If it does not move, call us before you need it.

Standards that decide how a toilet should perform

Vitreous china toilets are built to ASME A112.19.2, and EPA WaterSense labels toilets that use 1.28 gallons or less per flush while meeting performance tests. A toilet with a WaterSense label has been tested for flush performance, not only water savings.

The same code that governs the toilet requires a shutoff valve at each supply. WSSC recommends checking toilets twice a year with a dye tablet, which is a quick guideline to follow.
