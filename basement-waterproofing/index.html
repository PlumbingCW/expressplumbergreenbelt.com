Where basement water actually comes from

Surface water enters when the ground slopes toward the house, gutters spill near the foundation or window wells fill. Groundwater enters from below when the water table rises after heavy rain, pushing water through the floor joint and cracks.

Identifying which kind you have changes the fix. A grading and gutter correction can solve a surface problem for little cost. A groundwater problem needs a drain and a pump.

Rule out plumbing first

Not all basement water is groundwater. A leaking supply line, a failing water heater or a sewer backup can look the same. Before you spend on waterproofing, test for plumbing causes.

Our leak detection methods separate a plumbing leak from seepage. If the water has a sewage smell, stop and call. That is a drain problem and not a waterproofing one.

Greenbelt conditions

Greenbelt Homes, Inc. reports that few of its homes have basements, so basement waterproofing here is mostly a question for the ten block homes and twenty-five 1969 townhouses with basements and for newer homes in surrounding subdivisions. Crawl spaces in the older housing also benefit from drainage and moisture control.

Heavy rain in the Anacostia watershed can raise the water table quickly. Properties near streams and low ground are the most exposed. Check your gutters and downspouts first, because many wet basements in the area trace to a downspout that empties at the wall.

Before major excavation or changes to drainage, check with the City of Greenbelt about permits. Cooperatives may also have rules about exterior changes.

Mold, humidity and finishing a basement

Finishing a basement before the water problem is solved is the most expensive mistake homeowners make. Drywall, carpet and framing hold moisture, and mold can follow within days of repeated dampness. Solve the water first, then finish.

A dehumidifier helps control humidity once the space is dry. Aim to keep indoor humidity under about 50 percent. Use a hygrometer to check, since a space can feel fine while the air is damp enough to feed mold.

Efflorescence, a white powder on masonry, is a sign that water is moving through the wall and leaving minerals behind. It is not mold, but it points to the same moisture path. Wash it off, then watch whether it returns after rain.

Maintenance that keeps a dry basement dry

Clean gutters twice a year and extend downspouts several feet from the wall. Keep soil sloped away from the foundation, and keep mulch and plantings from blocking the slope. Test the sump pump every few months and replace the battery on a schedule.

Keep a record of dates and rainfall when water appears. That history helps us find the pattern, and it supports an insurance claim if your policy has a backup endorsement.

What the County's drainage manual says

The County's Residential Drainage Manual is the best local guideline for a wet basement. It says to grade ground away from the house on all sides, to keep gutters clear, to extend downspouts onto splash blocks or solid pipe and to contact DPIE before remedial work.

It describes a private drain as a gravel trench with perforated pipe at a minimum 1 percent slope, with access points every 50 to 100 feet, and says an enclosed drain is generally not advised without a suitable discharge point.
